Slide 01 // 0:00–0:45

5 GetMusic Growth Loops

How AI helps a solo founder operate built-in distribution without a team.

I'm going to talk about GetMusic, which is a side project I've been running for independent music discovery and Bandcamp code distribution.

The angle is not "AI built this for me." The angle is: if you're one person, you need the product itself to create distribution. Then AI can help you operate those distribution loops without hiring a team.

Set expectations early. This is about product design and operational leverage, not a tour of prompts.

Transition: first, give people enough GetMusic context to understand the loops.

Slide 02 // 0:45–1:25

What GetMusic does

Bandcamp artists, music fans, discovery, and recirculation.

Quick context: Bandcamp artists often have download codes. Those codes are basically free digital album redemptions, and artists want them to reach the right listeners.

Fans want discovery, not another infinite feed. GetMusic sits in the middle: it distributes codes, organizes releases, promotes artists, and keeps old catalog moving again instead of letting it disappear after launch week.

The logo is cute, but the system is pretty pragmatic: artist uploads create assets, assets create surfaces, surfaces create reasons to share.

Important line: GetMusic is not just a code database. It is a distribution machine built around artist activity.

Transition: before the mechanics, show that the loops are creating real dollars.

Slide 03 // 1:25–2:15

Revenue: slow, volatile, upward

Not a hockey stick. A proof of life.

I want to show revenue early because most side-project talks dodge the obvious question: is this actually working?

This is not a hockey stick. It's messy, slow, and volatile. But it is upward across more than three years. And there are no subscriptions or recurring billing here. Every dollar is usage-based.

The important part for me is that more than half of revenue comes from artists who have used it before. That means the product is not just acquiring one-off transactions. It creates reasons for artists to come back.

Use this as proof, not bragging. The graph says: loops can compound even when the business model is not SaaS recurring revenue.

Transition: now explain the frame behind the loops.

Slide 04 // 2:15–3:05

Product loop first. Automation second. AI third.

Distribution, automation, AI ops.

This is the key frame for the whole talk. I think a lot of AI product conversations start in the wrong place. They ask, "Where can I add an LLM?"

I think the better question is: what repeated product events create useful raw material? In GetMusic, those events are uploads, profiles, social handles, spotlights, catalog pages, radio links, and notifications.

Automation handles the plumbing: jobs, queues, schedules, Buffer and Make flows, review workflows. AI is the ops layer on top. It writes summaries, social copy, tag descriptions, playlist blurbs, and moderation checks from structured inputs.

The order matters. If the product does not create the loop, AI just creates more chores.

Transition: walk through the five loops, starting with the simplest: an upload.

Slide 05 // 3:05–4:00

Loop 1: Uploads -> Distribution

A release upload becomes a release page and a promotional post.

When an artist uploads a release, that upload is not the end of the workflow. It is the start of the distribution loop.

The release gets a page. The code distribution is set up. A summary can be generated. Social copy can be queued. The release enters the catalog for future resurfacing.

This is the first loop because it is the easiest one to miss. In many products, user input gets stored and forgotten. Here, the product action creates a reusable asset.

Takeaway: design product actions so they create reusable distribution assets.

Transition: the release is one asset. The artist profile is another.

Slide 06 // 4:00–4:50

Loop 2: Artist metadata becomes attribution and reach

Artist profile data becomes distribution context.

Artists give GetMusic names, genres, links, social handles, locations, bios, and release context. Most products treat that as profile decoration.

For GetMusic, it becomes distribution infrastructure. If we know the artist's handle, the post can tag them. If we know the genre, the release can appear in the right catalog surfaces. If we know the profile, the release is attached to an artist page that gives attribution and context.

That attribution matters because artists are much more likely to engage when the product makes them visible, not anonymous.

Every piece of user-provided metadata can become future distribution context.

Transition: once you have attribution, notifications become growth triggers.

Slide 07 // 4:50–5:40

Loop 3: Notifications create re-share moments

Operational updates become social triggers.

Artists get notified when GetMusic does something meaningful for them: welcomes them, features them, finds airplay, or includes them in a promotional surface.

A notification is not just an operational update. It tells the artist, "There is something here you might want to share." That turns internal product events into external amplification moments.

This loop is about timing. If you tell someone at the moment they have something to be proud of, they are far more likely to share it than if you ask for promotion generically.

If the product creates a moment someone is proud of, tell them exactly when it happens.

Transition: the strongest version of that is when the artist helps create the content themselves.

Slide 08 // 5:40–6:35

Loop 4: Spotlights turn artists into co-marketers

Artist-provided answers become feature stories and social threads.

Spotlights are artist features built from structured interview answers. The artist supplies the raw material: origin story, creative process, release context, what they want listeners to know.

GetMusic turns that into a polished feature. Then the feature becomes social copy, a thread, and a page that the artist has a reason to share.

The incentive is different from normal marketing. I'm not asking an artist to promote my platform in the abstract. I'm giving them a well-presented story about their own work.

The artist is not just the subject of the content. They helped create the raw material, which makes them more likely to distribute it.

Transition: the fifth loop is about extending beyond one obvious release page.

Slide 09 // 6:35–7:25

Loop 5: IndieCrates extends the surface area

Tag pages, playlist pages, crates, and genre copy create more places for music to appear.

A release should not only live on its own page. It should show up wherever its context makes it relevant: tags, genre pages, playlists, crates, radio matches, and other discovery surfaces.

IndieCrates is one example of that. It gives GetMusic another surface where catalog can be recirculated. The screenshot here shows the same principle through tag and player surfaces: structured catalog data becomes another discovery path.

This is where AI helps a lot, because every surface needs language: descriptions, summaries, blurbs, and social posts. But the surface has to exist first.

A release onboarded years ago can still create distribution today if the product keeps generating surfaces around it.

Transition: after the five loops, explain the actual AI operating layer.

Slide 10 // 7:25–8:40

AI as the missing tiny team

Automations and AI-assisted jobs let me operate the site in 1–2 hours a week.

This is where AI actually fits. I do not have an editor, social manager, genre copywriter, playlist writer, blogger, moderator, or trust-and-safety reviewer.

Instead, I have product events, Rails jobs, prompts, queues, and review points. AI handles the first draft or first pass for release summaries, platform-specific posts, tag descriptions, playlist descriptions, featured post drafts, and offensive-material checks.

The reason this works is not that the model is magic. It works because the product creates structured inputs: artist name, release title, genres, Bandcamp metadata, profile links, quote fields, tag context, and the surface where the output will be used.

AI is useful here because it is operating inside a loop with clear inputs, outputs, and review boundaries.

Transition: close with the strategic lesson.

Slide 11 // 8:40–9:45

The hard part is the loop, not the LLM.

If AI helps everyone build features faster, defensibility moves to the system around the product.

The biggest lesson from GetMusic is that the hard part is not calling the LLM. That keeps getting easier.

The hard part is designing the loop: what product event happens repeatedly, what raw material it creates, what automation catches it, what surface it flows into, who has an incentive to share it, and how it comes back into the product.

If AI can help anyone build features faster, the defensible part is the loop around what the product does. The LLM helps operate the system. It does not replace the need to design the system.

Don't ask, "Where can I add AI?" Ask, "What repeated product events create useful raw material?"

Transition: quick thank you, point people to slides and notes.
