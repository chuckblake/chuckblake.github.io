Slide 02
0:45–1:25

What GetMusic does

  • Bandcamp artists have download codes and releases they want listeners to find.
  • Fans want discovery without another infinite algorithmic feed.
  • GetMusic sits in the middle: distribute codes, organize releases, promote artists, recirculate catalog.
  • The important idea: artist activity creates assets, assets create surfaces, surfaces create reasons to share.
Slide 03 · Revenue proof
1:25–2:15

Revenue: slow, volatile, upward

  • Show revenue early to answer: is this actually working?
  • Not a hockey stick: messy, slow, volatile, but upward across 3+ years.
  • No subscriptions or recurring billing; revenue is usage-based.
  • More than half comes from returning artists — proof the product creates reasons to come back.
  • Use this as credibility for the loops, not as a brag slide.
Slide 04 · Frame
2:15–3:05

Product loop first. Automation second. AI third.

  • Wrong starting question: “Where can I add an LLM?”
  • Better question: what repeated product events create useful raw material?
  • Distribution layer: uploads, profiles, handles, spotlights, referrals, catalog pages, radio links, notifications.
  • Automation catches the events; AI turns structured inputs into copy, summaries, review checks, and descriptions.
  • If the product does not create the loop, AI just creates more chores.
Slide 05 · Loop 1
3:05–4:05

Artist inputs create attribution and sharing

  • Artist data is not profile decoration; it is distribution infrastructure.
  • Names, handles, genres, bios, links, and story answers decide who can be tagged and what can be promoted.
  • Spotlights are the same loop with more raw material: the artist gives context, GetMusic packages it into a feature and a thread.
  • The artist has a natural reason to share because the content gives them attribution and makes them visible.
  • Takeaway: every useful artist input should become future distribution context.
Slide 06 · Loop 2
4:05–4:55

Uploads → Distribution

  • An artist upload is not the end of the workflow; it is the start of distribution.
  • Upload creates a release page, code distribution, catalog entry, and social promotion opportunity.
  • The screenshot pair shows the loop: product page on one side, public post on the other.
  • Takeaway: design user actions so they create reusable distribution assets.
Slide 07 · Loop 3
4:55–5:45

Notifications create re-share moments

  • Notifications turn internal product events into external amplification moments.
  • Examples: welcome, featured artist, airplay notice, playlist inclusion.
  • Timing matters: tell artists when they have something to be proud of.
  • This works better than asking generically for promotion.
Slide 08 · Loop 4
5:45–6:35

Referrals turn artist goodwill into signups

  • This is the new explicit growth loop: share a link, another artist signs up, the referrer earns credits.
  • The reward is native to the product. Credits mean more release distribution, not a random coupon taped onto the side.
  • The ask is simple: if GetMusic helped promote your music, invite another artist who needs the same thing.
  • Automation can handle attribution, crediting, reminders, and follow-up copy; the loop works because the incentive is already in the product.
Slide 09 · Loop 5
6:35–7:25

IndieCrates extends the surface area

  • A release should not only live on its own page.
  • Catalog context lets music appear in tags, genre pages, playlists, crates, radio matches, and players.
  • IndieCrates is another surface where GetMusic catalog can recirculate.
  • AI is useful because every surface needs language: descriptions, blurbs, summaries, and posts.
  • The surface has to exist before AI can help fill it.
Slide 10 · AI ops layer
7:25–8:40

AI as the missing tiny team

  • No editor, social manager, genre copywriter, playlist writer, blogger, moderator, or reviewer.
  • Instead: product events, Rails jobs, prompts, queues, and review points.
  • AI handles first drafts and first-pass checks for summaries, posts, descriptions, featured drafts, and moderation.
  • The reason it works: structured inputs and bounded outputs.
  • Practical result: the site can be operated in roughly 1–2 hours a week.
Slide 11 · Closing lesson
8:40–9:45

The hard part is the loop, not the LLM.

  • Calling the model is getting easier and less defensible.
  • The hard part is designing the repeated event, raw material, surface, incentive, and return path.
  • AI operates the system; it does not replace the need to design the system.
  • Memorable close: do not ask “Where can I add AI?” Ask “What product events create useful raw material?”
